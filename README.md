# Elimu Learner Success & Risk Report
### Summary
This report integrates marketing acquisition data with academic retention outcomes to identify the core drivers of student attrition (the gradual reduction of students over time) at Elimu Business School. 
Currently, the institution faces a baseline dropout rate of 31.56%. By analyzing demographic, financial, and academic engagement data, we have identified predictable risk patterns that allow the institution to shift from reactive tracking to proactive intervention.

---

### The Core Drivers of Attrition 

#### 1. The Compounding Debt Trap -Financial Risk

Financial strain is rarely a sudden event.
It is a compounding issue where learners accumulate financial friction without the cussioning of a scholarship, their probability of dropping out statistically skyrockets.

**The Critical Zone:** Students who are behind on current tuition, possess historical debt, and have no scholarship drop out at a staggering rate of 87.3%.

**The Scholarship Buffer:** By contrast, students who hold scholarships have near zero finance related dropout risk, showing off a graduation rate of 75.9% and a dropout rate of only 12.4%.

#### 2. The Time Poverty Crisis -Age Demographics

Student retention is heavily dictated by age and external life pressures. Older demographics are not leaving because the coursework is too difficult but because of the overwhelming friction of balancing full-time jobs, families, and rigid academic schedules.

**The Professional Age group:** The 27-35 age cohort experiences a massive 57.0% dropout rate.

**The Traditional Advantage:** Younger students in the 17-21 age bracket have a significantly lower dropout rate of just 21.4%.



#### 3. Success vs Volume -Marketing ROI

Treating all marketing leads equally is resulting in wasted budget and inflated dropout numbers. Sourcing channels with low user intent yield high attrition.

**High Risk Channels** 

   **Social media** and **Search engines** yield the highest dropout rates at 35.8% and 34.6%, respectively. These digital channels generate impulsive enrollees who are frequently poorly prepared for actual academic workload.

**High Quality Channels**

   **Direct referrals** produce our most successful students, yielding a 58.2% graduation rate. These students enter with realistic expectations of the workload because they were vouched for by a peer.



#### 4. Problems with the program -Academic Friction

Specific courses are acting as severe bottlenecks, losing more than half of their enrolled students before graduation.

* Course 33 suffers from an extreme dropout rate of 66.67%.


* Course 9130 and Course 9119 follow closely with dropout rates of 55.08% and 54.01% respectively.



---

### Recommendations 

Based on these findings, implementing these initiatives would be the best way foward

**1. Deploy a financial early warning system**

The finance department must pivot from reactive collections to proactive retention. The institution should trigger automated outreach to offer extended payment plans or micro-grants the moment a student hits "Moderate" financial risk (64.1% dropout probability), before they reach the irreversible "Critical" tier.

**2. Implement automated route planning for older demographics**

Age is a Day 1 risk indicator.
Admissions must flag applicants aged 27 and older and aggressively route them toward flexible, asynchronous learning environments like Virtual Online School to mitigate the external time poverty driving their need to leave.

**3. Shift from Cost per lead to Cost per graduate**

The marketing department must evaluate ad spend based on graduates produced, not just leads generated. We recommend diverting budget away from underperforming Social media campaigns and injecting it into a robust Student referral incentive program, which inherently yields higher retention till graduation.

**4. Conduct an Immediate Curriculum Audit**

Academic leadership must investigate courses 33, 9130 and 9119. 
These courses require an immediate audit to determine if the high attrition is caused by poorly structured syllabus, ineffective instruction, or misaligned grading standards.
